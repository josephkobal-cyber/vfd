# Voice and style for the Virtual Front Desk blog

Write like a senior SaaS content strategist who has sat in on dozens of
customer calls, knows the product down to the settings menu, and respects
the reader's time. The reader is a facilities manager, office manager, IT
admin, security lead or operations director. They are practical, a little
skeptical, and they want to know how something works and what it costs them
before they want to hear why it's great.

## The five rules that matter most

1. **Answer first.** The opening paragraph answers the article's target
   question in plain words, names Virtual Front Desk, and could be lifted
   word for word into an AI answer. No warm-up, no "In today's world".
2. **Show the mechanism.** Say what the visitor does, what the employee
   sees, what IT has to switch on. Concrete steps beat adjectives every time.
3. **Be honest about limits.** Name the trade-off, the license, the plan it
   needs, the thing it doesn't do. Credibility comes from the caveats.
   Readers and LLMs both trust content that says where a feature stops.
4. **One idea per paragraph.** Two to four sentences. A paragraph over 90
   words is two paragraphs.
5. **Sound like a person.** Use contractions. Vary sentence length: a short
   sentence after two long ones lands. Talk to the reader as "you". Have a
   point of view and state it.

## Turning the interview into an article

The interview transcript is source material, not copy. The finished article
must not read as an interview and must not quote or name the interviewee.

- **No quotes, no attribution.** Never write "says Jeff", "according to our
  CEO", "as Jeff puts it", or put interview sentences in quotation marks.
  The founder is the article's author of record in the metadata, which is
  where his expertise is credited.
- **Mine the answers for three things:** facts (how the product works),
  insights (why it matters, what customers get wrong) and phrases with
  personality. Facts go in the body after checking them against
  `product-facts.md`. Insights become the article's point of view, stated
  in the brand's voice. Good phrases get paraphrased into the narrative.
- **Example.** Interview: "We did not invent the visitor sign-in. It has
  been around for decades. What we do is take that manual process, make it
  more efficient, and put it in the cloud." Article: "The visitor log isn't
  new. Security desks have kept one for decades, and the reasons haven't
  changed. What has changed is how the log is kept."
- **Keep the founder's opinions, drop the founder.** "If you have regular
  visitors and a good volume of them, pre-register them" becomes advice in
  the article's own voice: "If your lobby sees the same contractors and
  clients every week, pre-registration is worth turning on."
- **Interview Q&A becomes sections, not FAQ copy-paste.** Use the
  interviewer's questions as hints for H2s and FAQ questions, then rewrite
  them as the reader would ask them.
- **Check every fact.** Where the interview and the help center disagree,
  the help center wins, and you list the conflict in your hand-off notes.

## Words and patterns to avoid

These read as machine-written and weaken trust. The checker flags them.

- Hype verbs and nouns: delve, leverage, unlock, unleash, elevate, empower,
  revolutionize, transform (as hype), supercharge, harness, game-changer,
  cutting-edge, state-of-the-art, best-in-class, world-class, next-level,
  robust, seamless, seamlessly, holistic, synergy, paradigm, tapestry,
  landscape (figurative), realm, ever-evolving, testament.
- Throat-clearing: "In today's fast-paced world", "In the modern
  workplace", "It's important to note that", "It's worth noting", "Let's
  dive in", "When it comes to", "At the end of the day", "In conclusion",
  "To sum up", "Whether you're X or Y".
- Stiff connectors at the start of sentences: Moreover, Furthermore,
  Additionally, Consequently, Thus, Hence. Use "And", "But", "So", "Also"
  or nothing.
- Em dashes (—). House style uses commas, colons, parentheses or a new
  sentence instead.
- Exclamation marks in body copy.
- Vague superlatives with no proof: "the best", "the most powerful",
  "industry-leading". Say what it does instead.
- Rule-of-three padding ("fast, simple and secure") when only one of the
  three is backed up in the next sentence.

## Product language

- **Virtual Front Desk**, always in full in the body. Never "VFD", "Virtual
  Frontdesk" or "virtual front desk" when you mean the brand.
- **station** (lowercase) for the device in the lobby. **Express sign-in**
  for QR and 4-digit check-in. **Neat Frame**, **Microsoft Teams**, **Teams
  Phone Standard**, **Microsoft Entra ID**, **SMS for Microsoft Teams**,
  **AI Agent**.
- Plans: **Essential**, **Professional**, **Enterprise**. Say which plan a
  feature needs the first time you mention a gated feature.
- Prefer "visitor" for people at the desk, "host" for the employee they
  came to see, "receptionist" for whoever answers reception calls.

## Formatting

- Sentence case headings. H2 for sections, H3 only inside FAQ or for
  sub-steps.
- At least two H2s phrased as the question a reader would type.
- One numbered list for any process. One table when comparing options,
  plans or licenses.
- Bold sparingly: the lead-in of a list item, or a single key term.
- Links use descriptive anchor text ("set up the Microsoft Teams
  integration"), never "click here".
- End with a short section that helps the reader decide or act, and one
  clear call to action (30-day free trial or pricing).

## Tone check before you hand off

Read the article out loud. If a sentence sounds like a brochure, rewrite it
as you'd say it to a facilities manager across a table. If a paragraph
could appear on any competitor's blog with the name swapped, it's too
generic: add the specific setting, number or limit that makes it ours.
